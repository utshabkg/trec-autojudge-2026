\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{The two judges do best on different truth measures}

\judge{NuggetRecall} is far ahead on short answers. On rag25-gen it reaches about
0.79 Kendall on both truth measures. \judge{Corroborate} stays at 0.26 to 0.32.
One reading is that a good short answer stands out by stating precise facts that
most teams left out. A consensus bank, built from claims that teams share, cannot
reward such facts by construction. When designing \judge{Corroborate}, we worried
that consensus would pull every system toward the median. This result fits that
worry. Other causes could explain it too, such as how few sentences a short answer
has.

On long reports the picture depends on the truth measure. On ragtime25 Nugget
Coverage, \judge{NuggetRecall} reaches 0.697 with Qwen3-30B and 0.728 with
gpt-oss-20b, close to the median of 0.726. \judge{Corroborate} gets only 0.598. On
F1 the order flips. \judge{Corroborate} reaches 0.662 Kendall and 0.524 \tauap,
against medians of 0.583 and 0.364. \judge{NuggetRecall} reaches 0.47 to 0.52.
If F1 has a precision side, as its name suggests, it penalizes padding in a way a
recall measure does not. So does \texttt{NPREC}, since every sentence that no other
team corroborates lowers it. TIRA reports only the maximum over our measures, so we
cannot confirm which \judge{Corroborate} measure produced its F1 score.

The two judges also order systems differently. On our first pilot runs, Kendall
between \texttt{NCOV} from \judge{Corroborate} and \texttt{NR\_VITAL} from
\judge{NuggetRecall} is 0.51 on ragtime25 and 0.25 on rag25-gen. Between
\texttt{NPREC} and \texttt{NR\_VITAL} it is only 0.20 and $-$0.05. Inside
\judge{NuggetRecall}, the four mean-based measures agree with one another at 0.87
to 0.97 on both pilots.

TIRA keeps the best of a judge's measures. One leaderboard carrying the measures of
both judges would therefore have matched the better judge on every truth measure.
That gain would come from the scoring rule rather than from better judging. We
submitted the judges as separate systems instead.

\subsection{The judge model matters most}

With the prompts held fixed, swapping the LLM moved the score more than anything
else we tried. The comparison is not fully controlled, though. These runs also
differ in code version, reply budget and server
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}). On rag25-gen Strict Vital Score, Qwen2.5-7B reaches
0.604. Qwen3-30B and gpt-oss-20b both reach 0.783. In the organizers' run of our
code, gpt-oss-120b reaches 0.839. We leave dragun-repgen out of this comparison,
for the reasons in Section~\ref{sec:dragun}.

The 7B model does not fail on format. Over 5,302 calls it never returned an
unreadable reply. It just judges less accurately. On the synthetic dataset, it gave the worst run 38\% recall. Qwen3-30B gave
that same run 0\%. That suggests the smaller model grants partial credit too
freely. We did not count its labels by type on the pilots.

Our reasoning model, gpt-oss-20b, helps more on long reports. On ragtime25 Nugget Coverage it beats Qwen3-30B by 0.031 Kendall and
0.036 \tauap. It wins even though 2.9\% of its judgments stayed unreadable and 5 of 122 topics got a title-only key. Checking twenty facts against a long report seems to reward a model
that thinks first. On F1 the two are within noise (0.517 against 0.500). On short
answers they tie on Strict Vital Score Kendall at 0.783. On \tauap\ there,
gpt-oss-20b is lower (0.601 against 0.621). On Sub Coverage gpt-oss-20b is behind
on Kendall (0.736 against 0.788) and slightly ahead on \tauap\ (0.622 against 0.615).

\subsection{DRAGUN cannot rank LLM judge settings}
\label{sec:dragun}

Our first \judge{NuggetRecall} run on dragun-repgen used Qwen3-30B and scored
0.141. The organizers' runs of our code scored about 0.59 with Qwen2.5-7B and with
gpt-oss-20b. With gpt-oss-120b they scored 0.804. We made six more runs to find out
why (Table~\ref{tab:dragun}).

The code is not the cause. With Qwen3-30B, the current code scored 0.041.

The input does differ. Unlike the release, the copy on TIRA gives the judge only the article's headline (Section~\ref{sec:task}). The answer keys from the organizers'
runs show this, since their title field holds the headline on all 30 topics. Once
we rebuilt headline-only topics locally, the two copies behaved alike. Our
Qwen2.5-7B run on headlines agrees with the organizers' Qwen2.5-7B run at 0.73
Kendall on \texttt{NR\_VITAL}. Our run on the released topics agrees at 0.28. Our
\judge{Corroborate} run on headlines at floor 0.50 reproduces the organizers'
\run{lead-racer} leaderboard exactly, with Kendall 1.000 on every measure.

Matching the input did not close the gap. On headlines our Qwen2.5-7B run scored
0.190, while the organizers' run of the same setup scored 0.587. Our Qwen3-30B
run scored 0.054. Two runs of one setup can differ this much because every run
writes new answer keys. Here only 2 of 30 keys came out identical to the
organizers'. They can also differ because the pilot has only 28 systems. On the 12
runs the release marks as priority, our Qwen2.5-7B ranking agrees with the
organizers' at only 0.36 to 0.79 Kendall, depending on the measure.

So dragun-repgen cannot tell us which model or setting suits \judge{NuggetRecall}.
Across our runs and the organizers' runs, its scores for this judge range from 0.04
to 0.80. The 0.804 of gpt-oss-120b comes from a single run. \judge{Corroborate} is
deterministic, so its scores there are stable from run to run. One lesson carries
over to any track. Check that the data you validate on locally is the data the
evaluation platform feeds your judge. On rag25-gen the two copies differ only in
which field holds the request, a case our judges happen to handle. On
dragun-repgen they differ in substance, which we found out only after the deadline.
TIRA does not let participants download runs on the test sets, so we could not make the
same check there. Both DRAGUN copies keep the article's id as the request id. A
judge could therefore look up the original article in the release's topics file
and use it as context. We did not try this.

\subsection{Ranking within each topic}

We also re-aggregated the identical Qwen3-30B judgments with \texttt{NR\_RANK}, the within-topic percentile of Equation~\ref{eq:percentile}. On rag25-gen this raises Kendall from 0.783 to 0.792 on Strict Vital Score
and from 0.788 to 0.809 on Sub Coverage. It also raises \tauap\ by 0.013 on both.
The change needs no extra LLM calls. Since the judgments are identical, the
run-to-run noise of Section~\ref{sec:noise} does not apply to this comparison.
On ragtime25 the rank does worse, with 0.681 against 0.697 on Nugget Coverage
and 0.465 against 0.500 on F1. One possible reason is that a rank keeps only the
order of runs on a topic, while on long reports the size of the gaps between runs carries information. The
reported score is a maximum, so emitting both forms costs nothing. Our
\run{nuggetrecall-rank} systems do exactly that.

\subsection{Averaging two LLMs helps at the top, on one truth measure}
\label{sec:ensemble}

Our ensemble ranks runs within each topic by the \texttt{NR\_VITAL} of each model.
It then averages the two percentiles with equal weight. No weight was fitted. On
rag25-gen Strict Vital Score its \tauap\ is 0.695, 34th of 385 entries. That
compares with 0.634 for Qwen3-30B (the better of its two rows) and 0.601 for
gpt-oss-20b alone. Two judges that make different mistakes seem to cancel some of
each other's errors about the best systems. On Sub Coverage, though, the ensemble
is worse than either model on \tauap, at 0.562 against 0.628 and 0.622. On
ragtime25 it never beats gpt-oss-20b alone, since averaging dilutes the stronger
judge.

We chose the ensemble for rag26 under the earlier scoring. That scoring reported
one maximum over both rag25-gen truth measures, so it showed only the gain. Under
the current scoring the gain holds for one truth measure and reverses for the
other. Our other rag26 systems hedge that choice.

\subsection{Length is a weak shortcut under the current truth}

The organizers' length baseline ranks systems by how long their responses are.
Under the current truth measures it reaches 0.152 Kendall on rag25-gen and 0.193 on
ragtime25. Under the earlier scoring it reached 0.558 on ragtime25. We spent design
effort keeping our measures from rewarding length, which LLM evaluators are known
to do~\cite{dubois2024length}. Length explains far less of the revised ragtime25
truth measures. We do not know which part of the change caused that.
So whether length looks like a strong shortcut depends on how the truth is computed
as well as on the track. A judge checked against one truth computation should be
checked again against the other.

\subsection{An inert threshold and what fixing it did}
\label{sec:floor}

\judge{Corroborate} filters claims by how specific they are to the topic. It
measures this as the cosine similarity between the embeddings of the claim and the
topic. Claims below a floor are dropped. We first set that floor to 0.15 and swept
it from 0 to 0.20. Every value gave the same bank, which we read as a sign that the
parameter did not matter. We then measured what the encoder actually produces on
hand-written sentences about a made-up topic. Specific facts scored 0.587 to 0.753
and boilerplate hedges (generic caveats) 0.361 to 0.472. Even nonsense sentences
scored 0.266 to 0.319. The floor and its whole sweep sat below all of these, so the
filter kept everything on every dataset. That did real harm. A hedge is exactly
what every team writes, so hedges took top places in a bank sorted by team count.

We moved the floor to 0.50, between hedges and facts. We also added a warning for
when a filter keeps everything or nothing. At submission time, the pilots showed no
difference between the two floors under the scoring then in force. The scores were
0.323 against 0.323 on rag25-gen and 0.690 against 0.691 on ragtime25. So we kept
0.50, because it makes the filter do its job. Our submitted \judge{Corroborate}
systems use it.

The current numbers are less kind. On ragtime25 F1 our score fell by 0.009. On
dragun-repgen, with the same released topics, Kendall fell from 0.755 to 0.739 and
\tauap\ from 0.608 to 0.516. \judge{Corroborate} is deterministic, so this is the
effect of the floor and not run-to-run variation. Still, on a pilot with 28
systems, a drop in \tauap\ of 0.09 can come from a few pairs near the top. The
organizers' run at 0.50 saw headline topics (Section~\ref{sec:dragun}) and scored
0.734 and 0.505, so the topic text mattered far less than the floor. What carries
over is the check rather than the new floor. Report the range of values a
threshold actually sees and warn when it never fires. A sweep over a dead
parameter looks exactly like a robust one.

\subsection{Run-to-run noise}
\label{sec:noise}

vLLM does not produce
bit-identical output when the mix of requests in a batch
changes~\cite{he2025nondeterminism}. As a result, the same configuration run twice
gives slightly different judgments. We measured the size of
this noise by accident. On ragtime25 we re-ran Qwen3-30B with one measure added,
which cannot lower a maximum. The re-run also had a larger reply budget, which no
Qwen3-30B reply had reached. The score still fell by 0.010 under the earlier
scoring. Under the current one it fell by 0.014 on Nugget Coverage and by 0.019 on
F1, while \tauap\ moved by 0.001 and 0.007. Still, one re-run says little about a measure that leans on a few top systems, so \tauap\ differences are less certain than Kendall ones. We therefore treat Kendall differences
below 0.02 as noise on rag25-gen and ragtime25 and claim none of them. On
dragun-repgen, with far fewer systems, the spread is much larger
(Section~\ref{sec:dragun}). We report no significance tests. No comparison in this
paper is a claim of beating another entry.
